
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Session types~\cite{DBLP:conf/concur/Honda93,DBLP:conf/parle/TakeuchiHK94} provide a typing framework for structured communication
among concurrent processes.
The idea is to specify, for each communication channel,
the protocol that processes are expected to follow on it.
In this paper we focus on \emph{binary} session types,
where each channel is expected to be used by exactly two processes
following a common protocol.
A number of type systems have been proposed to verify that
processes do in fact respect the protocols.
These systems aim to provide two main properties:
\emph{session fidelity}
  (that actions observed at each channel do follow the protocol),
and
\emph{deadlock freedom}
  (that there is always some process able to take the next action).
While very important for applications, deadlock freedom is especially challenging
to achieve in a compositional way.
The classic issue is that processes can introduce potentially circular dependencies in the way different channels are used, and such dependencies
are not reflected in the types of single channels.
Historically, there have been two main approaches to solve this issue:
one based on priorities, and one based on Curry-Howard correspondences.

The priority-based approach, introduced in Kobayashi's seminal paper \cite{Kobayashi97}
and developed in a series of other works, \eg \cite{Padovani14,DardhaG18},
essentially adds information to the cross-session dependencies in the types
and requires some global, non-compositional, analysis
to detect if the dependencies are circular.

In this paper, we focus on the Curry-Howard-based approach,
pioneered by~\citet{DBLP:conf/concur/CairesP10,DBLP:journals/jfp/Wadler14},
which established a connection between linear logic and session types.
The promise of the approach is to obtain guarantees on the behaviour
of the processes from the consistency of the logic.
%
The main idea which supports deadlock freedom is that the logical
correspondence imposes some constraints on the communication topology
of the system, which
\begin{enumerate*}[(1)]
  \item can be enforced compositionally, and
  \item make circular dependencies impossible to construct.
\end{enumerate*}
\citet{DBLP:conf/concur/CairesP10,DBLP:journals/mscs/CairesPT16}
derive a typed \picalc\ from \emph{dual intuitionistic linear logic},
which is shown to ensure session fidelity and absence of deadlocks.
\citet{DBLP:journals/jfp/Wadler14} proposed CP, a concurrent calculus
derived from \emph{classical linear logic},
which enjoys deadlock freedom;
\citet{DBLP:journals/jfp/Wadler14} further connects CP with ``standard'' session
types systems by showing that CP can embed GV,
a variation of the calculus previously proposed in \citet{DBLP:journals/jfp/GayV10}, which enriches a concurrent \lcalc\ with
linear types and session-typed primitives
(but that originally admitted deadlocks).
Subsequently, \citet{conf/esop/LindleyM15} completed the picture by
providing an elementary proof of deadlock freedom for CP, and translations from and to GV.

While these type systems satisfy the goal of \emph{compositionality},
% (and can be automated)
they only promise behavioral guarantees, like deadlock freedom,
for \emph{closed} programs;
the meaning of general typing judgments in terms of the component's behavior
is left completely implicit.
The technique of \emph{logical relations} has been developed with the
specific goal of filling this gap (\citet{TimanyKDB24} provide a modern discussion).
Logical relations are predicates over the behavior of a program,
indexed over types and type environments, which make it possible to define
a \emph{semantic} counterpart $\senv \proves \proc : \ty$
to the \emph{syntactic} judgment $\senv \types \proc : \ty$.
While the syntactic judgment can, by definition, only be established by
using the syntactic type derivations,
the semantic judgment can be proven to hold by any means necessary,
by reasoning directly at the level of the possible behaviors of the component.
This approach enables the safe composition of typable components,
with components which respect the interface specified by the types,
even if the type system would reject them.

Consider, for example, a syntactically typable process~$\proc$ expecting to receive
a choice between~$\asTy{A}$ and~$\asTy{B}$
on some channel~$\ch : \asTy{A} \echoice \asTy{B}$;
we might want to compose it with a component~$\asPrId{elect}$
that makes the choice by internally running a leader-election protocol
and letting the leader pronounce their choice on~$\ch$.
Such an election protocol might require violating the strict invariants
imposed by the syntactic type system, like acyclicity of topology or absence of races; yet, if this behavior is handled correctly internally,
it should be safe to compose it on $\ch$ with a safe context.
If a logical relation encoding the safe behavior expected on $\ch$
was available, one could prove
$\ch:\asTy{A} \echoice \asTy{B} \types \proc : \ty$ syntactically,
and
$\ch:\asTy{A} \ichoice \asTy{B} \proves \asPrId{elect} : \tyUnit$ semantically,
and conclude that their composition over $\ch$ is deadlock-free.
The objective of this paper is identifying such a logical relation.

\citet{DBLP:conf/esop/PerezCPT12,DBLP:journals/iandc/PerezCPT14} were
the first to apply logical relations to session types,
where they are used to investigate strong normalization, confluence, and behavioral equality.
\citet{conf/esop/RochaC23} prove strong termination and correct use of
shared state using logical relations for
an extended calculus with session types and shared references.
In all these
works~\cite{DBLP:conf/esop/PerezCPT12,DBLP:journals/iandc/PerezCPT14,conf/esop/RochaC23},
logical relations are formulated as means to prove further properties \emph{on
  top of} subject reduction and deadlock freedom (progress), 
which are proven syntactically.
As a result, the logical relations are not strong enough to justify the type
system's soundness (and deadlock-freedom) from first principles,
rather they only apply to typed processes,
making it impossible to use them as a semantic typing judgment.

A series of works~\cite{DBLP:journals/lmcs/HinrichsenBK22,DBLP:conf/cpp/HinrichsenLKB21,DBLP:journals/pacmpl/HinrichsenBK20}
introduced Actris,
a program logic based on the Iris framework~\cite{JungKJBBD18},
which integrates a (dependent) session type style assertion language
with separation logic resources to support expressive manual reasoning
for a language mixing advanced features (\eg higher order, heap) and
message passing.
The approach is genuinely semantic, allowing the safe composition
of untypable-but-safe components.
For our purposes, the Actris approach is too limited because it is only able to
prove safety of programs, not deadlock freedom.
The work that comes closest to our goals is LinearActris~\cite{JacobsHK24},
which uses a linear instantiation of Iris to prove deadlock freedom
for a language using a fragment of Actris' protocols without impredicative invariants.
The approach is semantic, but relies on imposing rather strict
constraints on the communication topology as a global invariant
of every program, using connectivity graphs~%
\cite{DBLP:journals/pacmpl/JacobsBK22,DBLP:journals/pacmpl/JacobsB23}.
While sufficient to justify Curry-Howard-based typing,
LinearActris would rule out our leader election example (and many others)
which is deadlock-free and safe to compose,
but violates these topological invariants.

\paragraph{Contributions}
In this paper we consider a variation of GV (dubbed~\OurLang)
and propose a new logical relation for it which is:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Compositional:}
        it validates all the rules of a standard Curry-Howard-based
        session type system;
  \item \textbf{Semantic:}
        it expresses the meaning of a judgment entirely as constraints
        on the behavior of untyped processes;
        and
  \item \textbf{Liberal:}
        it does not constrain the communication topology of components,
        as long as they are well-behaved on their external interface.
\end{enumerate}

We articulate our main technical ideas in
a concurrent \lcalc\ enriched with
core session typed communication primitives:
we define a semantic judgment $\senv \proves \proc : \ty$
through our logical relation, and prove that it satisfies
all the rules of a standard GV-like type system.
We further prove that the semantic judgment implies
safety and deadlock-freedom of closed processes.

While previous work correctly identified
topological invariants imposed by Curry-Howard-based syntactic type systems,
our results show that
they are not \emph{necessary} to establish correctness of typing,
and a much weaker invariant is in fact sufficient:
the topological invariants need to be enforced \emph{only at the external interface}. We show that this can be made to work in the presence of mobility,
by requiring some discipline for internal channels that become part of the external interface, on the fly as they get sent to the environment.

To evaluate the approach, we extend the core calculus with
branching, recursion, unrestricted channels and linking,
showing that the approach scales to more complex features.
We also provide examples which would not be admissible in
systems based on constraining the communication topology,
but satisfy our logical relation.
